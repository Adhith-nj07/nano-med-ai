Background & methods

What this tool is, and what it isn't

Written for anyone, regardless of chemistry or machine-learning background.

What "stability" and "reactivity" mean here

Colloidal stability

When nanoparticles are mixed into a liquid, they can either stay evenly spread out (stable) or clump together into larger clusters over time (unstable). Clumped particles behave very differently — they may settle out, stop working as intended, or move through the body differently.

Biological reactivity

An illustrative estimate of how strongly a body's cells or immune system might respond to the particle — for example through inflammation or stress on cells. It is a teaching signal only, built from general chemistry rules, not from any biological or toxicology testing.

This is not a medical, safety or regulatory tool. NanoMedAI's models were trained on synthetic data — particle outcomes generated from chemistry rules of thumb plus random noise, not from laboratory measurements. The models mostly reproduce those rules, not real nanoparticle behavior. Do not use this tool to make formulation, safety, dosing, or clinical decisions. It has not been validated against real experiments.

How the prediction is made

Two models — one for stability, one for reactivity — are "random forests": each is 100 simple decision trees that each learned a rough rule of thumb from the training data. For a given particle, every tree votes on the most likely outcome, and the votes are averaged into the percentages you see. The trees run directly in your browser; no data is sent to a server.

The rules the training data was built from: PEG coatings tend to add protection against clumping; high salt reduces the electrical repulsion that keeps particles apart; a pH close to a material's "isoelectric point" encourages clumping; heat speeds up particle collisions; bare (uncoated) surfaces tend to attract proteins and trigger more reaction; smaller particles tend to be more reactive; and materials that release ions (silver, zinc oxide, copper oxide) tend to be more reactive than inert ones (gold, silica).

Why size changes how a particle behaves

More surface, less volume

Shrinking a particle sharply increases the fraction of its atoms sitting at the surface rather than buried inside. That's why nanoparticles are often far more chemically active, gram for gram, than the same bulk material.

Smaller dissolves faster

Curved, small surfaces are thermodynamically less stable, so smaller particles tend to dissolve faster than larger ones of the same material — part of why nanoparticles are used to improve how well poorly-soluble drugs absorb.
